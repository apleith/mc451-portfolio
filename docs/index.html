Abstract

This study examines how digital games portray the theory of evolution through natural selection (ENS), the cornerstone of the biological sciences. Using quantitative content analysis of 22 evolution-themed digital games (14 commercially distributed, 8 academically developed for education or research), this study addressed two research questions: (1) what proportion of evolution-themed games contain none, some, or all three principles of ENS — phenotypic variation, differential fitness, and fitness heritability — and (2) does the completeness of ENS representation differ between commercial and academic games? Findings indicate that only 5 of 22 games (22.7%) presented all three principles, with phenotypic variation by far the most commonly depicted single principle. A Chi-Square test of independence comparing ENS completeness against game origin produced a non-significant result; however, several expected cell counts fell below the conventional threshold of 5, limiting the inferential strength of the test. These findings support the broader argument that the interactive properties that make digital games engaging — particularly direct player control over outcomes — are inherently in tension with the automatic, non-agentive process of natural selection.

0.0.1 About This Study

This portfolio presents a content analysis of digital games that represent biological evolution. The study traces which scientific principles of evolution through natural selection (phenotypic variation, differential fitness, fitness heritability) appear in commercially distributed video games versus games developed by educators and researchers. The motivating question: when a teacher reaches for a “fun” game to introduce evolution, are those games actually teaching the science correctly?
