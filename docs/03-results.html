4  Results

Phase IV · The Analyst

This section presents the findings of the content analysis (N = 22 games). Results are organized to address each research question in turn. Descriptive statistics are presented first, followed by inferential analysis.

4.1 Descriptive Statistics

4.1.1 Overview of the Sample

The sample comprises 22 evolution-themed digital games. Just over six in ten (63.6%) were commercially distributed games discoverable through standard video-game databases; the remaining 36.4% were academically developed games identified only through targeted web searches and personal-network outreach. This imbalance is itself a finding: the commercial-channel discoverability of academic evolution games is substantially weaker than that of mainstream commercial titles.

4.1.2 Distribution of Primary Variable: ENS Completeness

Table 2 addresses RQ1 directly. The dominant category is Partial representation (15 of 22 games, 68.2%) — games that present one or two of Lewontin’s three ENS principles but not all three. Only 5 games (22.7%) presented the full set of principles required for ENS to actually occur. Two games (9.1%) — both academically developed information-presentation titles — presented none of the three principles in their game mechanics, despite their explicit framing as evolution education.

4.1.3 Bar Chart: Distribution of ENS Completeness

Figure 1 visualizes Table 2. Two patterns stand out. First, Partial representation is the modal category by a wide margin — most evolution-themed games depict some but not all of the principles required for natural selection to function as a closed system. Second, the Complete category is sparse: only 5 of 22 games (22.7%) clear the bar of representing all three Lewontin principles in their mechanics.

4.1.4 Distribution by Individual Principle

Table 3 disaggregates ENS Completeness into the three principles. Phenotypic variation appears in 20 of 22 games (90.9%) — by far the most commonly depicted principle. Differential fitness and fitness heritability appear in only 6 (27.3%) and 8 (36.4%) games respectively. This pattern suggests that game designers reach naturally for visible diversity (different-looking organisms) but more rarely simulate the consequence-and-inheritance machinery that turns variation into actual evolution.

4.2 Inferential Analysis

4.2.1 Chi-Square Test of Independence: ENS Completeness × Game Origin

This Chi-Square test addresses RQ2: does the completeness of ENS representation differ between commercially distributed and academically developed games?

Table 4 displays the cross-tabulation. The two None games are both academic; commercial games have at least one principle in every case. The Complete category is split — 3 commercial games (Creatures, SimEarth, SimLife) and 2 academic games (Selection Game, Who Wants to Live a Million Years) — and the Partial category is dominated by commercial games (11 of 15).

Chi-Square Result:

A Chi-Square test of independence was conducted to examine the relationship between ENS Completeness and Game Origin. The result was not statistically significant, χ²(2) = 4.14, p = 0.126.

Three observations on the Chi-Square result:

  1. The test was not statistically significant at α = .05 (p = 0.126). The data do not provide evidence of a relationship between game origin and ENS completeness. Both commercial and academic games appear roughly comparable in the proportion of titles that present all three Lewontin principles.

  2. All academic games rated as “None” (n = 2) belong to a specific subtype — quiz-based information-presentation titles (Angry Aliens: Evolution and Life Preservers) that teach evolution as content rather than simulating it as a process. The qualitative significance of this pattern is not captured by the inferential test alone.

  3. The minimum expected cell count was 0.73, which falls below the conventional threshold of 5 required for the Chi-Square test of independence. Three of the six expected cell counts in this 3×2 table are below 5. This is a consequence of the small sample size (N = 22) and the rarity of the Complete and None categories. The Chi-Square result should therefore be interpreted with substantial caution — Fisher’s Exact Test or a substantially larger sample would be required to test this relationship rigorously. The original published study used qualitative thematic analysis precisely because the available population of evolution-themed games was too small to support inferential statistics.

4.3 Summary of Findings

NoteKey Findings at a Glance

  • RQ1 (How complete is ENS representation in evolution-themed games?): The dominant category is Partial (15 of 22 games, 68.2%). Only 5 games (22.7%) present all three Lewontin principles. Two games (both academic, both information-presentation quiz titles) present none of the three principles in mechanics.

  • RQ2 (Does completeness differ between commercial and academic games?): The Chi-Square test of independence was not statistically significant. However, expected cell counts below 5 in three of six cells limit the inferential strength of this test substantially. The data do not provide statistical evidence of a relationship; the small sample size precludes confident conclusion either way.

## Inferential Analysis

### Chi-Square Test of Independence: ENS Completeness × Game Origin

This Chi-Square test addresses RQ2: does the completeness of ENS representation differ between commercially distributed and academically developed games?

```{r}
#| label: tbl-crosstab
#| tbl-cap: "Table 4. Cross-tabulation of ENS Completeness by Game Origin (N = 22)."

cross_tab <- table(coding_data$ens_completeness, coding_data$game_origin)

cross_tab |>
  as.data.frame.matrix() |>
  rownames_to_column(var = "ENS Completeness") |>
  kable(align = "lcc") |>
  kable_styling(
    bootstrap_options = c("striped", "hover", "condensed"),
    latex_options = c("striped", "hold_position"),
    full_width = FALSE,
    font_size = 13
  ) |>
  add_header_above(
    c(" " = 1, "Game Origin" = ncol(cross_tab))
  ) |>
  row_spec(0, bold = TRUE, background = "#b71c1c", color = "white")
```

Table 4 displays the cross-tabulation. The two **None** games are both academic; commercial games have at least one principle in every case. The **Complete** category is split — 3 commercial games (*Creatures*, *SimEarth*, *SimLife*) and 2 academic games (*Selection Game*, *Who Wants to Live a Million Years*) — and the **Partial** category is dominated by commercial games (11 of 15).

```{r}
#| label: chi-square-test
#| include: false

chi_result <- suppressWarnings(chisq.test(cross_tab))

chi_x2  <- round(chi_result$statistic, 2)
chi_df  <- chi_result$parameter
chi_p   <- round(chi_result$p.value, 3)
chi_sig <- ifelse(chi_result$p.value < .05, "statistically significant", "not statistically significant")
min_expected <- round(min(chi_result$expected), 2)
```

**Chi-Square Result:**

A Chi-Square test of independence was conducted to examine the relationship between ENS Completeness and Game Origin. The result was `r chi_sig`, χ²(`r chi_df`) = `r chi_x2`, *p* = `r chi_p`.
